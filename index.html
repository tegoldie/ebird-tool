Sydney Bird Finder

About the tool

This tool is designed for Sydney-based birders to help them choose where and when to go to see rare birds. The data comes from checklists submitted by ordinary birders to eBird. It covers locations identified by eBird as hotspots – popular places to go birding.

The tool gives each hotspot a “Rare Birds Score” highlighting how often rare species are seen there. For each hotspot, the tool displays which rare species are found there and how often.

The rarer a species is, the more the tool will highlight it at hotspots where it appears. This means you’ll know which species might make the hotspot worth the trip.

Access to birding hotspots can vary, so google the area before you grab your binoculars and get in the car.

Rare Birds Score

Each hotspot receives a Rare Birds Score outlining how often rare species are seen there. More specifically, the score measures:

How many species might you record on a trip to this hotspot that do not appear on any of 20 randomly selected checklists from the dataset?

The tool makes adjustments for what time of year checklists were submitted and for differences in observers’ recording patterns.

For each hotspot, the tool displays species that contribute at least 0.1 to its Rare Birds Score, either for the selected month or for All Year. This means that the tool will be quick to display rare species like the Glossy Black Cockatoo or Leaden Flycatcher at many of the hotspots where they have been recorded.

On the other hand, common birds like the Golden Whistler, Spotted Pardalote or Dusky Moorhen won’t usually be picked up in the tool. Common species are a charming and rewarding part of bird watching, but you might not need this tool to find them.

The Sydney region

This tool is intended for Sydney-based birders, so it focuses on hotspots within driving distance of Sydney.

The Region filter lets you set how wide to cast the net, out to the full coverage area or zooming in on a single subregion:

  • Greater Sydney and Illawarra – places you can generally reach for early morning birding from central Sydney, including the Blue Mountains and Central Coast. This is set as the default.
  • Greater Sydney and surrounds – the widest option, adding further-flung areas including the South Coast and Hunter Valley.
  • Choose subregion – zoom in on one area, such as the ACT.
Months and seasons

Birds move with the seasons, and knowing when to search for a species is often as important as knowing where. For each hotspot, the tool tracks which birds are around throughout the year and how the hotspot’s Rare Birds Score rises and falls with them. This means you can see the best time to visit.

Orange flags

The data used by this tool is observations submitted by ordinary people to eBird. Observers visit hotspots inconsistently and record in different ways, so the data can be patchy or skewed. The tool tries to correct data issues, but this cannot be done perfectly. For this reason, the tool should be used as a guide rather than a perfect prediction.

Where the tool identifies a specific concern, it adds an orange flag to encourage caution, for example when a hotspot has a low number of checklists.

Night Mode

Default Day Mode is designed for daytime birding. A separate Night Mode lets you explore records of nocturnal species like owls and Tawny Frogmouths.

Night records are much more sparse, so Night Mode shows where species have been reported and when, without estimating probabilities or tracking seasonal changes.

Nocturnal species can be recorded roosting during the day, so they also appear in default Day Mode. Similarly, Night Mode can include daytime species, particularly on checklists that overlap dusk or dawn.

The tool uses the eBird Basic Dataset for New South Wales and the Australian Capital Territory. eBird is a citizen science platform for ordinary people to report their bird observations. Users submit a checklist of the birds they identified at a particular time and place. eBird identifies popular birding locations as hotspots, which is where most checklists are submitted.

The tool should be treated as providing a useful guide, not a precise prediction. Uncertainty can be created by poor sampling of particular places or species. Observers may distort the data in various ways, for example by using personal local knowledge or only reporting a checklist when they see something interesting. The tool uses various checks to try and mitigate these issues, but this will always be imperfect.

Excluded data

Checklists are excluded if:

  • They duplicate an outing already represented in the dataset, including shared group outings (see Group outings below)
  • They are submitted to a location other than an eBird hotspot within the geographic scope of the tool
  • They are not marked as a complete checklist, meaning the observer has indicated that they reported all species they could identify
  • Their start time is missing or invalid, or they start earlier than three hours before local sunrise or at or after local sunset (see Night Mode)
  • They have no records remaining after the individual-record exclusions below

Individual records on checklists are excluded if:

  • They are of an introduced species
  • They do not name a species, for example “duck sp.”

Checklists that remain after these exclusions form the dataset for default Day Mode. Unless stated otherwise, “the dataset” means these checklists across the full coverage area. Night Mode uses a separate, overlapping selection (see Night Mode).

Hotspots are only displayed in default Day Mode if they have at least five checklists. Checklists at hotspots with fewer than five still contribute to calculations across the dataset and subregion.

Group outings

When members of a group share an eBird checklist, their copies can differ. Day Mode uses one eligible member’s copy for the outing, even if another copy includes additional species. It selects by checklist ID, rather than by the longest species list. The outing receives the highest known observer effect among the group members (see Observer effects). Night Mode instead combines species across qualifying copies (see Night Mode).

The Sydney region

Each hotspot is assigned to a subregion. Subregions are custom geographic units created from 2021 Australian Bureau of Statistics boundaries. The boundaries used by the Australian Bureau of Statistics are hierarchical, going from the smallest unit, SA1s, all the way up to the largest, SA4s. Most subregions in this tool align to SA4s, with some SA3s and one SA2 reassigned to create more logical boundaries around Sydney.

Rare Birds Score

Whole dataset probability

Each species is assigned a whole dataset probability: the chance that a randomly selected checklist from the dataset records the species. This is the observed proportion of checklists, without adjustments for observers or seasons.

whole dataset probability =checklists across Greater Sydney and surrounds reporting the species÷ all checklists across Greater Sydney and surrounds

Rarity weighting

Each species has one rarity weighting. This applies consistently across the whole tool. It does not vary between hotspots.

rarity weighting = (1 - whole dataset probability)20

This is the probability that the species does not appear on any of 20 independently selected checklists from the dataset. Hotspots with more checklists have more influence on whole dataset probability than hotspots with fewer checklists. A species that is widespread mainly in less-visited places can therefore receive a higher rarity weighting.

The figure of 20 checklists is a subjective design choice. You can think of it as representing a particular level of birding experience: roughly, the chance that the species is not yet on your life list after 20 trips across the region. This is an analogy; real birding trips are not random selections from the dataset.

Hotspot probabilities – All Year and Monthly

Each species is given a hotspot probability for each month at each hotspot. Months and seasons explains how these are calculated, including the adjustment for observers’ recording patterns (see Observer effects).

These monthly probabilities are averaged to calculate an All Year probability for the species at that hotspot.

All Year probability = sum of twelve monthly probabilities ÷ 12

Species score contribution

A species score contribution is calculated for each species at each hotspot.

species score contribution = All Year probability × rarity weighting

A species has a large score contribution when it is uncommon generally but frequently reported at the hotspot.

Rare Birds Score for each hotspot

A Rare Birds Score is calculated for each hotspot:

Rare Birds Score = sum of all species score contributions

The Rare Birds Score is used by the tool to rank hotspots.

All species with calculated probabilities contribute to the score, including those below the species display thresholds. The model only calculates a species’ probability at a hotspot if it has been recorded there.

Monthly scores

Species score contributions are also calculated for each month at each hotspot:

monthly species score contribution = monthly probability × rarity weighting

Adding these contributions gives the monthly Rare Birds Score for the hotspot:

Monthly Rare Birds Score for the hotspot= sum of monthly species contributions

Display thresholds and common species

Species with a score contribution of at least 0.10 in either the current view or All Year appear in the main species list. Other species with a contribution of at least 0.05 in the current view appear under More species.

The way rarity ratings are calculated means that many common species will never appear by default in the main species list of any hotspot. There are 55 species in this category, for example the Satin Bowerbird and Chestnut Teal. The tool works like this because it is designed to focus on rare and unusual birds that occur in fewer places. You can still explore any species, including more common ones, using the “Choose species” feature. Each hotspot also has a link to the corresponding eBird page which will show a full bird list.

“Show best hotspots only” limits the results to hotspots scoring at least 3.5 in the selected view.

Observer effects

Some observers regularly record more species than others. They may spend longer looking, recognise more calls, know where to search or submit different kinds of lists. A hotspot visited mainly by these observers can look better than a comparable hotspot visited mainly by people who tend to record fewer species.

The tool estimates an observer effect for each observer: how many species they tend to record compared with other observers at the same places and times of year. It uses these effects to put hotspots on the same footing. The displayed probabilities assume you record with an average level of thoroughness across the dataset. Observers who submitted more checklists contribute more to that average. Your own chances may be higher or lower, depending on how you bird.

How observer effects are estimated

The tool compares how many species observers report on checklists submitted by a single observer. Some checklists report more species simply because of where and when they were made: a hotspot may be richer in spring than in winter. The comparison allows for each hotspot, and for each calendar month within each subregion. The consistent differences associated with each observer give their observer effect.

Observers with only a few checklists don’t give much evidence on their own. Their observer effect is pulled towards what is typical for observers who submit a similar number of checklists.

eBird allows people who go birding together to share a checklist. The tool counts each shared outing once and uses the highest known observer effect among its members. Members’ copies can differ; Group outings in the Data section explains which copy is used.

Different species respond differently

A more thorough observer makes a bigger difference for some species than others. A skilled observer is far more likely than a beginner to pick up a quiet, skulking bush bird by call, but not much more likely to notice a pelican.

So the tool also estimates a species response for each species: how strongly the chance of recording that species rises with the observer effect. Where a species has little data, its species response is pulled towards the typical response across all species.

How observer effects impact scores

The observer effect and species response combine into an observer weight for each species on each checklist. This lets the model allow for who submitted a checklist, whether or not they recorded that species.

Observer weights affect the monthly probabilities (see Months and seasons), which in turn determine Rare Birds Scores.

The average level of thoroughness is calculated separately for each species, because differences between observers matter more for some species than others. The calculation details below explain how the model sets this average.

Observer effects

What the model does. The model explains the number of species on each checklist as the combined result of three things: where it was made (the hotspot), when it was made (the calendar month) and who made it (the observer effect). Because many observers visit the same hotspots in the same months, the model can compare them like for like. Group checklists which can be attributed to more than one person are not used for this calculation.

The formula. The model works with the logarithm of the species count:

log(species count)=hotspot effect+subregion–month effect+observer effect+unexplained variation

Using the logarithm means the effects work as percentages rather than fixed numbers of species. An observer whose effect is 0.1 higher than another’s records about 10% more species on a comparable checklist, whether the hotspot usually produces 20 species or 60. (Strictly, e0.1 ≈ 1.105, or 10.5% more.)

Observers with little data. Each observer effect has two parts:

observer effect=γ×log(checklist activity)+observer’s own deviation
  • The first part is what is typical for observers with the same checklist activity. γ is how observer effects change with checklist activity, estimated from the data. Submitting many checklists isn’t assumed to mean skill. The data decide how much it matters.
  • The second part is how far this observer differs from that typical value. It is pulled towards zero, more strongly the fewer solo checklists the observer has. An observer with many solo checklists keeps most of their own deviation. An observer with none gets the typical value.

How strongly to pull, and the value of γ, are estimated from the data using a standard method called restricted maximum likelihood.

What an observer effect isn’t. It isn’t a skill rating, a measure of time spent, or a personal chance of success. It is the observer’s usual recording pattern, inferred from their checklists.

Group checklists. A checklist shared by several observers counts once. It gets the highest observer effect among the group members whose effects are known, not the sum of their effects. There is no extra allowance for group size.

Species responses

What the model does. For each species, the model asks whether observers with higher observer effects record it more often than other observers at the same hotspot in the same calendar month. This reduces the influence of differences in where and when people go birding.

The formula. The chance that a solo checklist records the species is:

P(record)=1-e-H  where  H=e hotspot–month baseline+b×θ

where θ is the observer effect and b is the species response. H is the encounter rate: the number the model converts into a recording probability using 1 − e⁻ᴴ (see building block 2, encounter rates). Each extra unit of observer effect multiplies H by eᵇ. A species response of 0 means observer effect makes no difference for that species.

Avoiding circularity. If the same checklist were used both to estimate an observer’s effect and to estimate how a species responds to it, the two would reinforce each other. A checklist that recorded a rare species would push up its observer’s effect, and also make the species look more responsive to observer effect. To avoid this, the checklists are split into five groups, keeping each observer’s checklists from the same date together. When estimating species responses, each checklist’s observer effect comes from a version of the model fitted without its own group. This is called cross-fitting.

Minimum data. A species gets its own estimate only if there is enough information to compare observers:

  • at least 100 solo checklists and at least 5 records of the species, counting only
  • hotspot–month combinations with at least 15 solo checklists, where some checklists recorded the species and some didn’t.

Combinations where every checklist, or none, recorded the species say nothing about differences between observers, so they are left out.

Uncertainty. Each species’ estimate comes with a measure of how uncertain it is. That measure allows for the fact that one observer’s checklists tend to resemble each other, so they aren’t treated as fully independent evidence. A minimum level of uncertainty is also applied, so that no estimate is treated as more precise than the model can justify.

Pulling estimates towards the typical response. The final species response combines each species’ own estimate with the typical response across all species:

  • A species with a precise estimate keeps most of its own value. A species with an uncertain estimate is pulled further towards the typical response.
  • The result is the average of the plausible values for the species once both sources of information are combined. It isn’t produced by simply setting negative estimates to zero.
  • A species without enough data for its own estimate gets the typical response across all species.

This approach, where the group average is estimated from the data and then used to steady individual estimates, is called empirical Bayes.

Observer weights

For each species, the model first converts the observer effect into a multiplier. It then divides that multiplier by its average across the dataset to give the observer weight:

w=e b×θaverage of e b×θ across all checklists in the coverage area

where θ is the observer effect and b is the species response. Each checklist counts equally in the average, so observers with more checklists contribute more. The calculation is separate for each species, and its weights average 1.

The displayed probabilities use an observer weight of 1: the average multiplier for that species. This is what “average thoroughness” means here. The model averages the multipliers after converting the observer effects; it does not take the average observer effect first or identify one person who represents the average for every species.

A weight of 2 gives the same chance of recording the species as two independent visits by someone with a weight of 1, at the same hotspot and time of year. It does not double the probability. If one such visit gives a 30% chance, a weight of 2 gives a 1 − (1 − 0.3)² = 51% chance.

For a species with a response of 0, every checklist has a weight of 1, so observer effects make no difference to that species.

What the adjustment doesn’t capture

The observer weight reflects an observer’s usual pattern. It doesn’t adjust individual checklists for their duration, distance travelled, weather, the habitat searched, or the time of day within the permitted window. The adjustment also can’t fully allow for targeted searches for a particular bird, unusually productive visits, or an observer’s particular familiarity with a hotspot.

Months and seasons

The tool estimates probabilities on a monthly basis for each species at each hotspot. It does this in two stages, first by estimating the pattern, for example when the species is most common and how strong the contrast is between different months; and then by estimating the level, meaning how much to scale the pattern up or down based on how common the species is at the hotspot.

The pattern is estimated using a combination of hotspot- and subregion-level data. Generally, the more data there is at the hotspot level, the less the model needs to draw on the subregion level data.

The level is estimated based on how many checklists recorded the species, allowing for when they were submitted and by whom. Dollarbirds may have the same pattern of summer arrival and winter absence in two hotspots, but may still be more common on average in one hotspot compared to another.

By combining the seasonal pattern with the level, the model is able to estimate a probability for each month. The tool displays a bar chart of monthly probabilities for each species at each hotspot.

Where data is poor at both the hotspot and subregion level, most often for rare species, the model pulls all months towards the yearly average, since the evidence for seasonal variation is weak.

Seasonal flags

The tool displays a badge showing months where the species’ probability is at least 60% of its highest monthly probability, for example “Seasonal: Oct–Feb”. A period can continue across December and January. One or two separate periods can be shown; if more than two qualify, only the period containing the peak is shown. There is no badge when every month reaches 60%, including when all months are equal.

The seasonal pattern provided by the model is a best guess and there is a lot of variability in the quality of data informing the pattern. In cases where the pattern is more uncertain, the tool displays a “seasonal pattern uncertain” orange flag (see Seasonal pattern uncertain orange flag, below).

The steps in more detail

Step 1: Reading the hotspot level data

As a first step, the model uses checklists from the hotspot to estimate how the species’ pattern changes through the year:

  • Each month is combined with the months either side. All the checklists from the three months are pooled together, so months with more checklists count for more. This reduces statistical noise.
  • The calculation allows for who submitted each checklist, using the observer weight for this species (see Observer effects).
  • After combining neighbouring months, the model adjusts for gaps in the data. Where fewer of those months have checklists, it pulls the estimate closer to the yearly average. A month without checklists provides no evidence about whether the species was there; a month with checklists but no records does.
  • The model then puts the twelve monthly estimates on a common scale, so they describe the pattern through the year separately from how common the species is overall. Step 4 sets that overall level.

The result of these calculations is the hotspot pattern, which might show, for example, that the species is most common in November and absent in June.

The model then looks at how reliable the hotspot data is by calculating hotspot reliability. This runs from 0 to 1 and measures whether the hotspot’s own data is enough to show the species’ pattern: plenty of checklists spread across the year, given how often the species is recorded there. More data will be required if the species is rare.

Step 2: Reading the subregion level data

Next the tool looks at data across the subregion. It asks whether the differences between months are larger than chance would normally produce. This check allows for when and where checklists were submitted, who submitted them, and the species being recorded more often at some hotspots than others.

If the check finds evidence of seasonal variation, the model uses a subregion pattern built from records at the other hotspots. The hotspot being analysed is left out. The pattern is built in the same way as the hotspot pattern, from all the other hotspots’ checklists combined.

If the differences could be explained by chance, the subregion pattern is flat, meaning every month is the same.

The model then calculates subregion reliability, which measures whether the subregion data is reliable, meaning plenty of checklists across the year at hotspots other than the one being analysed. More data is needed if the species is rarer. The model also checks whether the pattern stays about the same if any single hotspot or single year is removed.

Subregion reliability runs from 0 to 1. Small imperfections don’t count against the subregion pattern: its reliability only drops below 1 when the records fall short of a set standard, or when removing a single hotspot or year shifts the pattern noticeably.

Step 3: Blending the patterns

The final seasonal pattern is a blend of three patterns: the hotspot pattern, the subregion pattern and a flat pattern in which every month is the same.

To determine how this blend is calculated, the model compares the hotspot pattern with the subregion pattern. It asks whether the differences between them can be explained by chance, given the hotspot’s actual checklists, or whether the hotspot systematically differs from the subregion.

The result of this comparison decides which pattern leads:

  • If the difference could be explained by chance, the subregion pattern leads. It gets weight in line with subregion reliability. The remaining weight is shared between the hotspot pattern and the flat pattern: the more reliable the hotspot data, the more of it goes to the hotspot pattern.
  • If the hotspot clearly differs, the hotspot pattern leads. It gets weight in line with hotspot reliability. The remaining weight is shared between the subregion pattern and the flat pattern: the more reliable the subregion data, the more of it goes to the subregion pattern.

If the calculation cannot settle the comparison, the hotspot pattern also leads as a precaution. The calculation details explain this case and what happens when there is no subregion pattern.

This approach tries to identify cases where a particular place shows seasonal patterns that differ from the subregional average and treats them differently. The approach scales how the hotspot pattern and subregion pattern are used based on how reliable they are. To the extent neither is reliable, the model concludes that no pattern can be identified with confidence, and so falls back to the flat pattern.

Step 4: Setting the level

At this point, the model has identified a seasonal pattern for the species at the hotspot. The next step is to scale the pattern according to how often the species is recorded at the hotspot. The model picks the level at which, given the real checklists at this hotspot (when they were submitted and by whom), the tool would expect exactly as many records of the species as were actually made.

This provides a probability for every month, assuming you record with the average thoroughness described under Observer effects.

Seasonal pattern uncertain orange flag

The tool adds a “seasonal pattern uncertain” orange flag when the seasonal pattern rests on limited evidence.

This happens when the hotspot reliability is low (less than 0.5) and the model can’t confidently draw on the subregional data either because:

  • The hotspot clearly differs from the subregional pattern or
  • The data suggests the species has different seasonal patterns in different places, which the model observes by applying a subregion consistency test – see below
  • No subregion pattern can be estimated because no other hotspot has records of the species.

Details: the subregion consistency test

The subregion consistency test asks whether, for a particular species, more than one in ten of the well-sampled hotspots in the subregion have a hotspot pattern that is inconsistent with the subregion pattern. If this is the case, it suggests that the species does not have a consistent pattern across hotspots, and therefore that the subregion pattern can’t be relied upon in the absence of good data at any particular hotspot.

The test uses hotspots with reliability of at least 0.5 where the comparison with the subregion pattern can be completed. It fails if more than 10% of those hotspots are inconsistent, or if fewer than three can be compared. The calculation needs both patterns and a test result that settles whether they differ.

A hotspot counts as inconsistent with the subregion pattern only if it fails on two counts: the Step 3 comparison finds that it clearly differs, and its pattern is more than a distance of 1 from the subregion pattern (see building block 3 (comparing two patterns), below). Broadly speaking, the hotspot pattern is more different from the subregion pattern than moving the whole pattern a month later would make it. This means a difference has to be both statistically clear and reasonably large to count.

Building block 1: patterns

A pattern describes when a species is more or less likely to be recorded, without saying how likely. It is a set of twelve numbers, one for each month, that add up to one. A larger number gives a month more weight in the pattern.

The average monthly share is 1/12. A share of 2/12 gives a month twice that weight in the pattern, but does not necessarily double its recording probability. A share of 0 gives zero probability in that month. A flat pattern has 1/12 in every month.

A pattern is not a probability. A share of 1/12 does not mean an 8.3% chance of recording the species. Probabilities only appear in Step 4, when the pattern is combined with the level.

Building block 2: encounter rates

The encounter rate describes how readily the species is recorded. The model uses it to allow for differences between observers before converting the result into a probability. It estimates this rate from whether checklists recorded the species, rather than from counts of individual birds.

For encounter rate H and observer weight w for this species, the probability that the checklist records it is:

1-e-Hw

This conversion has two useful properties:

  • A weight of 2 has the same chance of recording the species as two independent visits with a weight of 1, under the same conditions (see Observer effects).
  • However high the encounter rate or the observer weight, the probability never goes above 100%.

The model estimates the encounter rate by finding the value that would produce the number of records actually made, given when the checklists were submitted and who submitted them. In Step 1 it does this for each group of three neighbouring months.

Building block 3: comparing two patterns

Steps 2 and 3 both ask whether two patterns differ by more than chance would produce. This takes two things: a way of measuring how different two patterns are, and a way of judging whether that difference is bigger than chance.

Measuring the difference. Picture each pattern as twelve piles of sand arranged in a ring. The distance between two patterns is the least amount of work needed to reshape one into the other: how much sand has to be moved, multiplied by how many months it has to travel. Because the calendar is a circle, a December peak is close to a January peak. Moving an entire pattern one month later produces a distance of at most 1.

For two patterns X and Y, the calculation keeps a running total of the differences between them, month by month:

cm=∑j≤m(Xj-Yj)  d(X,Y)=∑m|cm-median(c)|

where cm is the running total from January up to month m. Subtracting the median gives the same answer whichever month the running total starts from, so there is no artificial break between December and January.

Testing whether the difference is bigger than chance. Even if two patterns really are the same, their estimates will differ a little, because the records are a limited sample. To judge whether a difference is real, the model:

  1. sets up a scenario in which the difference being tested doesn’t exist. (This is called the null model.)
  2. simulates many sets of records under that scenario, using the real checklists: the same hotspots, dates and observers. Only whether each checklist records the species is simulated.
  3. builds a pattern from each simulated set in exactly the same way as from the real records, and measures its distance in the same way.
  4. counts how often the simulated distances are at least as large as the real one.

If simulated distances that large are rare, chance is an unlikely explanation for the real difference. This is expressed as a p-value:

p=number of simulated distances at least as large as the real one+1number of simulated sets+1

When the p-value is below 0.01, the model treats the difference as larger than chance would normally produce in the scenario being tested. This is evidence of a difference, not proof of a recurring seasonal pattern.

Some practical details:

  • Simulated sets with no records of the species are discarded, because there is no pattern to build from them.
  • Sets are simulated in batches, up to 999,999 sets with at least one record for the Step 2 test and 99,999 for Step 3. The calculation stops once a standard statistical range for the proportion of simulated distances at least as large as the observed one lies wholly above or below 0.01. This range is a 95% Wilson interval.

Two limitations are worth knowing:

  • The simulations assume the observer weights, and the encounter rates and levels used to set up each scenario, are correct. They don’t allow for uncertainty in those estimates.
  • The tool tests many species at many hotspots, so some differences will be flagged by chance. It does not adjust the 0.01 threshold for the number of tests. How often this happens also depends on whether the simulated scenarios describe the real data well.

Step 1 details: the hotspot pattern

Pooling

Each month is combined with the month before and the month after (December and January count as neighbours). All their checklists are pooled, so a month with many checklists counts for more than a month with few. The same month in all years is included together: January 2021 is combined with January 2022 and so on. Recent years do not get extra weight.

Encounter rate. For each month m, the model finds the encounter rate at which the expected number of records across the pooled checklists equals the actual number of records in them:

∑i∈pooled months[1-e-Hmwi]=records in the pooled months

This is the encounter rate conversion from building block 2 (encounter rates), applied to the three pooled months.

Months without checklists. Where there are gaps in the data, the model pulls the pattern towards the yearly average rather than assuming a particular seasonal pattern. Month support is the share of the three pooled months that have at least one checklist at the hotspot: 1 if all three do, ⅔ if two do, ⅓ if one does, and 0 if none do. The baseline B is the average encounter rate across the year, giving each month weight in line with its month support:

B=∑mamHm∑mam

Each month’s share of the hotspot pattern is then a mix of two things: its own encounter rate compared with the baseline, and the average (1). Month support sets the mix:

Sm=(1-am)+amHm/B12

So a month whose three pooled months all have checklists uses its own estimate entirely. A month with only one of the three keeps a third of its difference from average. A month with none is set to the average share, 1/12. Dividing by 12 makes the twelve shares add up to one.

The approach to months without checklists addresses gaps in the data, but does not address low sample sizes. Full month support can be provided by a single checklist in each of the three relevant months. Low sample size is dealt with in later steps, including by considering hotspot reliability.

Step 1 details: hotspot reliability

Hotspot reliability asks whether the hotspot’s checklists give the species enough chances to be recorded, in enough months, to show its pattern.

The model first estimates how often the species is recorded at the hotspot across all months, allowing for observer weights. For this calculation it assumes the species is equally likely in every month. It then works out how many records the hotspot’s actual checklists in each month would be expected to produce at that rate. Call this μₘ for month m. Each month scores between 0 and 1, and hotspot reliability is the average over the twelve months:

C=112∑mμmμm+0.5

A month with no checklists scores 0. A month expected to produce one record scores 0.67, and three records 0.86. Assuming the species is equally likely all year means a genuine migrant isn’t penalised for being absent in some months: the score measures the chances to record it, not the records themselves.

Because the expected number depends on how often the species is recorded, a rare species needs more checklists than a common one to reach the same reliability. If the species was recorded on every one of the hotspot’s checklists, each month’s number of checklists is used in place of μm.

The value 0.5 in the formula is a design setting.

Hotspot reliability measures whether the checklists provide enough chances to record the species across the year. It is not a probability that the pattern is correct, and it does not check whether the species returned in separate years.

Step 2 details: is there genuine seasonal variation across the subregion?

The model uses the method in building block 3 (comparing two patterns) to ask whether the species shows genuine seasonal variation across the subregion, or whether its ups and downs could be chance.

The scenario. The test imagines that the species has no seasonal variation anywhere in the subregion: at each hotspot it is equally likely in every month. Hotspots can still differ, though. The species may be recorded more often at one hotspot than another. So each hotspot gets its own year-round encounter rate, set from that hotspot’s own records over the whole year. This test uses every hotspot in the subregion, including the one being analysed.

The simulation. Using the real checklists, the model simulates records at each hotspot at its year-round rate, and builds a subregion pattern from each simulated set. These simulated patterns aren’t perfectly flat: chance, and the uneven timing of checklists across hotspots, create ups and downs. That is the point. They show how far from flat a subregion pattern can drift when there is no real seasonal variation.

The subregion test. This test runs only if the species has at least two records across the subregion and all fall within 120 days. It keeps the number of detections at each hotspot fixed and considers how they could have been distributed among that hotspot’s actual surveys, using individual observer weights. A flat temporal reference is fitted separately at each contributing hotspot, without a seasonal adjustment. The calculation then finds the probability that all those regional records would fall within any common 120-day period. Keeping separate hotspot totals allows occurrence rates to differ between places: surveys in other habitats do not manufacture evidence of exceptional timing. Hotspots without detections do not contribute to this conditional timing test.

The result. The species receives the same “one off” flag if either test gives a probability below 0.05. A successful subregion test applies the flag at every hotspot where the species was recorded. It does not cancel an existing local flag. For the local calculation, a rigorous upper bound may replace an expensive exact probability: if the bound is below 0.05, the true probability must be too.

The subregion pattern is built the same way as the hotspot pattern in Step 1, from all the checklists at the other hotspots combined. In effect, it treats the other hotspots as one large hotspot.

Step 2 details: subregion reliability

Subregion reliability is the lowest of three scores, each between 0 and 1. The first measures whether there are enough records. The other two measure whether the subregion pattern depends on a single hotspot or a single year. Taking the lowest score, rather than multiplying them together, avoids penalising the same problem twice.

Enough records. This works like hotspot reliability, across the other hotspots in the subregion. For each other hotspot, the model estimates how often the species is recorded there across all months, allowing for observer weights. Assuming the species is equally likely in every month, it works out how many records the hotspot’s checklists in each month would be expected to produce. Adding these across the other hotspots gives the expected records for each month, μₘ. Each month scores μₘ ÷ (μₘ + 2), and the average over the twelve months is I:

Rinfo=min(1, I/0.9)

A month expected to produce 18 records across the other hotspots scores 0.9, because 18 ÷ (18 + 2) = 0.9. The model treats an average score of 0.9 as full marks. If expected records are evenly spread across the twelve calendar months, about 216 across the dataset would therefore give a score of 1. These are totals across all years of data, not an annual requirement. The values 2 and 0.9 are design settings, chosen by the developer and checked with simulations and held-out data.

Stable when a hotspot is removed. The model rebuilds the subregion pattern with each other hotspot left out in turn, each time starting from the full data. Hotspots with no records of the species are included, because their checklists still affect the pattern. If the largest distance between the full pattern and any rebuilt pattern is Δsite:

Rsite=min(1, 1/Δsite)

Stable when a year is removed. The same, leaving out each calendar year in turn, with the largest distance Δyear:

Ryear=min(1, 1/Δyear)

Shifts up to a distance of 1 (see building block 3, comparing two patterns), roughly the size of moving the whole pattern one month later, carry no penalty. A shift of 2 halves the score, and a shift of 4 quarters it. The one-unit allowance is a design setting, not a statistical threshold.

Combining. Subregion reliability is the lowest of the three:

R=min(Rinfo, Rsite, Ryear)

Special cases. If no other hotspot has records of the species, no subregion pattern can be built and subregion reliability is 0. The seasonal warning then depends on hotspot reliability (see Seasonal pattern uncertain orange flag). If leaving out a single hotspot or year leaves no pattern to compare, that removal counts as a distance of 3, so subregion reliability cannot exceed one-third. This is a cautious setting chosen for missing comparisons, not a measured distance.

Subregion reliability is calculated from the other hotspots’ pattern even when the Step 2 test chooses a flat subregion pattern. It is not the probability that the subregion pattern is correct.

Step 3 details: comparing the hotspot with the subregion

The model uses the method in building block 3 (comparing two patterns) to ask whether the hotspot pattern differs from the subregion pattern by more than chance.

The scenario. The test imagines that the hotspot follows the subregion pattern exactly. It sets the level so that, given the hotspot’s actual checklists and who submitted them, the expected number of records equals the actual number.

The simulation. Using the hotspot’s real checklists, the model simulates records from the subregion pattern at that level, builds a hotspot pattern from each simulated set as in Step 1, and measures its distance from the subregion pattern.

The result. If the test finishes with a p-value of at least 0.01, the subregion pattern leads (A = 1). If it finds a clear difference, the hotspot pattern leads (A = 0). If the simulations cannot settle which side of 0.01 the result falls on, the hotspot pattern also leads as a precaution; that does not establish that the patterns differ.

Two safeguards also apply. If no subregion pattern can be estimated, the model uses the hotspot pattern mixed with the flat pattern according to hotspot reliability (A = 0 and R = 0). If a subregion pattern assigns zero probability to a month when the species was recorded locally, the model treats this as a clear difference and uses the hotspot-led blend. It does not discard those local records or give their month a zero prediction.

Step 3 details: the blend

The blend depends on the result of the comparison.

If the difference could be explained by chance, the subregion pattern leads:

q=R×P+(1-R)×[C×S+(1-C)×U]

If the hotspot clearly differs, or the comparison cannot settle the result, the hotspot pattern leads:

q=C×S+(1-C)×[R×P+(1-R)×U]

where q is the final pattern, S the hotspot pattern, P the subregion pattern, U the flat pattern, C hotspot reliability and R subregion reliability.

Step 4 details: the level

The level λ is set so that the expected number of records across the hotspot’s actual checklists equals the actual number, D:

∑i∈hotspot checklists[1-e-λ qmonth(i) wi]=D

where qmonth(i) is the final pattern’s share for the month of checklist i, and wᵢ is its observer weight for this species. The displayed probability for month m assumes an observer weight of 1 (see Observer effects):

pm=1-e-λqm

Because the level is set after the pattern, a change to the pattern can raise or lower the level, and with it the All Year value.

If every checklist with a positive expected encounter rate recorded the species, an exact match would need an infinitely high rate. In that case the target is reduced from n+ to n+² ÷ (n+ + 1), where n+ is the number of those checklists. The same rule is used when estimating the Step 1 encounter rates and the level in the Step 3 comparison.

Interpreting the estimates

A seasonal pattern combines records across years. It can reflect one concentrated episode rather than a season that repeats every year. The subregion checks reduce dependence on a single hotspot or year, but do not prove recurrence. Repeated visits to the same bird, targeted searches and changes in habitat can affect the results.

The model borrows seasonal patterns from the subregion, but sets each hotspot’s overall level from its own records. A hotspot with little data can therefore still have an uncertain overall probability. The tool does not display a range of uncertainty around each probability, and an orange flag is not a complete assessment of every possible problem.

For calculation speed, the model groups observer effects into 256 bands when estimating seasonal patterns and running the comparison tests. It uses the average observer weight for each species in each band. The final monthly probabilities and the one-off and decline assessments use individual checklist weights. Uncertainty in the estimated observer effects and species responses is not fully carried through to the displayed probabilities.

Orange flags

What flags do

Orange flags identify specific reasons to interpret one of the tool’s outputs cautiously. They do not change probabilities or scores.

Hotspot flags

  • Low sample – the hotspot has 30 or fewer checklists. Hotspots with fewer than five are not displayed in default Day Mode.
  • No checklists in the last 3 years – no checklists fall within the three years up to the data reference date, not the date you open the tool.
  • All checklists submitted by one observer – only one known observer appears across all the hotspot’s checklists in the dataset. There is no three-year restriction. Known members of shared groups count as observers.

Species level flags

There are three species level orange flags:

  • Seasonal pattern uncertain – meaning that the seasonal pattern displayed by the tool is subject to significant uncertainty
  • One off – meaning that the records for the species suggest it may have only been present at the hotspot for a single short period
  • Apparent recent decline – meaning that the species has been missing from many recent checklists to an extent that suggests it may now have a significantly lower presence at the hotspot than previously

Seasonal pattern uncertain

See Months and seasons

One off

Records of a species that are all bunched into one short period may mean the bird was only at the hotspot briefly, for example a vagrant that stayed for a few weeks, rather than being a regular visitor. The “one off” flag warns about this.

Bunching isn’t always a sign of a one-off visit. Records can bunch together because surveys were concentrated in one period, or because the species is a seasonal visitor. The local test asks how likely it is that all the hotspot’s records would fall within any 120-day period, allowing for its survey history, observers and the seasonal reference described below. The subregion test checks whether all records across the whole subregion form one short episode. Either test can supply the “one off” flag.

To judge when the species is usually around, the test uses records from other hotspots in the subregion (see Months and seasons, Step 2). It gives their seasonal pattern less influence when its regional reliability is low. If a pattern cannot be estimated, or assigns no chance of occurrence to a month with records at this hotspot, the test proceeds without a seasonal adjustment. It still accounts for when surveys occurred and differences between observers. This does not establish that the species occurs all year. The test does not use the hotspot’s own seasonal pattern, because the records being tested would help explain their own bunching.

A species can be flagged by the local test if it has at least two records at the hotspot, all within 120 days. It can also be flagged where every record across the subregion falls within one 120-day period and that regional concentration is unlikely. The subregion test requires at least two records in total; a hotspot with only one record can therefore receive the flag when the wider episode supplies the evidence.

The flag does not change the species’ probabilities or its score contribution. It is a warning only.

Calculation details

Which pattern is used. For the local test, let P be the other hotspots’ pattern from Step 2, R its existing regional reliability weight, and U the flat year-round pattern. For a usable P, the reference is R × P + (1 − R) × U. This retains the raw pattern at R = 1 and removes its seasonal influence at R = 0. If the raw P is undefined, or assigns zero opportunity to a month with local records, the reference is U instead. No part of the hotspot’s own seasonal fit enters this reference. The existing reliability measure does not establish recurrence across years.

Each checklist’s chance of a record. The test assumes the species follows the selected reference pattern at this hotspot. It sets the level so that the expected number of records across the hotspot’s checklists equals the actual number, as in Step 4 of the seasonality model. Each checklist then has a chance of recording the species that depends on its month (through the pattern) and on who submitted it (through its observer weight, see Observer effects).

Asking only about timing. The test takes the number of records as fixed and asks only when they fell. Out of all the ways that number of records could have been spread across the hotspot’s checklists, it works out how likely each one is. A set of checklists is more likely to be the one that recorded the species if those checklists fall in months when the species is more expected, or were made by more thorough observers. (Technically, each set’s probability is proportional to the product of its checklists’ odds of recording the species.)

Allowing for where the period falls. The 120-day period isn’t chosen in advance. It is wherever the records happen to fall. A test that only checked the observed period would be biased towards finding bunching, because any set of records looks unusually concentrated around where it actually lies. So the test adds up the probability of all the ways the records could fall within any 120-day period in the hotspot’s history. (Statisticians call this a scan test.)

The result. If that total probability is below 0.05, the species is flagged “one off”. Where the exact calculation would take too long, the tool can instead calculate a figure that is guaranteed to be at least as large as the true probability. If that figure is below 0.05, the true probability must be too.

Unavailable seasonal information. If the regional pattern cannot be estimated, or assigns no chance of occurrence to a month with local records, the local test uses the flat reference. It retains the actual checklist dates and individual observer weights. The subregion test always uses a flat temporal reference.

When a calculation cannot be completed. A numerical failure supplies no conclusion from that test. The other test can still supply a flag. Any seasonal warning comes from the separate checks under Months and seasons.

Limitations. If most of the hotspot’s checklists fall in the same short period as the records, there is little evidence about other times of year. The subregion test likewise accounts for survey dates at each contributing hotspot. Absence of a one-off flag does not establish regular occurrence. Sparse records or concentrated survey effort may leave insufficient evidence to distinguish a brief episode from continuing presence. Both tests use a fixed 120-day window and do not give additional weight to records being concentrated within a shorter period. The subregion test requires every regional record to fit inside one window: an older or scattered record outside it prevents that regional assessment, although local tests can still flag individual hotspots. The flag suggests a concentrated episode. It does not prove the bird will not return, and checklists from the same episode (for example, many people looking for the same bird) are not fully independent.

Apparent recent decline

This flag warns that a species which used to be a useful find at the hotspot may now be less likely there. It is based on the checklists submitted since the species was last recorded: if many later checklists missed it, at times of year when it would be expected, that suggests it may have declined at the hotspot. A long time since the last record isn’t enough on its own. There has to be evidence from later checklists.

The test asks: if the species were still present, but only just common enough to make the main species list, how likely is it that none of the later checklists would have recorded it? If that is unlikely (less than 10%), the tool flags an apparent recent decline.

The test is only run where all of the following apply:

  • the species is not flagged “one off”
  • its All Year score contribution is at least 0.15, comfortably above the 0.10 needed for the main species list
  • more than 730 days have passed since its last record, up to the data reference date
  • there are checklists at the hotspot since that last record.
Calculation details

The lower benchmark. The test reduces each monthly probability by the same proportion until the species’ All Year score contribution is 0.10, the threshold for the main species list. For month m, the benchmark probability is:

tm=min(1, pm×0.10/rp‾)

where pₘ is the displayed probability for month m, p̄ is the All Year probability (the average of the twelve monthly probabilities), and r is the rarity weighting. An All Year probability of 0.10 ÷ r gives a contribution of 0.10. The formula includes a cap at 100%, but the test only runs for contributions of at least 0.15, so it always scales probabilities down and the cap does not affect these cases.

Chance of no later records. Each checklist since the last record has a chance of recording the species at the benchmark level, allowing for its month and for who submitted it. Using the encounter rate conversion (see building block 2 (encounter rates)), a checklist with observer weight w in month m records the species with probability 1 − (1 − tm)w. The chance that none of the later checklists recorded it is the product of their individual chances of missing it:

∏later checklists i(1-tmonth(i))wi

The result. If this is below 0.10, the species is flagged.

Because the test uses the months of the later checklists, misses at times of year when the species is normally absent count for little. Misses by more thorough observers count for more. The test uses the final monthly probabilities from the seasonality model, so changes to that model can change which species are flagged, even though the test itself is unchanged.

Limitations. The flag doesn’t estimate how much a population has declined. It can also be triggered by a change in habitat, by people visiting different parts of the hotspot, or by later checklists that aren’t fully independent of each other.

Night Mode

Night Mode is for exploring checklists made at night, and records of nocturnal species such as owls, frogmouths, nightjars and owlet-nightjars. For each hotspot it shows how many checklists have reported each species, and when it was last reported.

Night Mode shows where birds have been reported and how recently, without estimating probabilities, scores or seasonal patterns. Its checklists are much fewer and are often submitted after targeted searches, so it does not apply the probability model used in default Day Mode.

Night Mode puts nocturnal species first. Owls, frogmouths, nightjars and owlet-nightjars are shown at every hotspot where they have been reported. Hotspots are ordered by how many different nocturnal species have been reported there.

Daytime species are also recorded on many night checklists, especially around dusk and dawn. These are listed in the collapsible More species section for each hotspot.

The numbers count checklists, not individual birds.

Which checklists are included

Night Mode includes checklists that start either:

  • one hour before local sunset or later; or
  • before local sunrise.

Only the start time matters. A checklist that starts before the evening cut-off isn’t included, even if it continues into the night, and a checklist that starts before sunrise is included, even if it continues into daylight.

Sunrise and sunset are calculated for each hotspot’s location and each date, using Sydney local time, including daylight saving.

Unlike default Day Mode, Night Mode also includes:

  • incomplete checklists, where the observer has not confirmed that they reported every species they identified
  • incidental checklists, eBird’s category for sightings made when birding wasn’t the main activity, such as an owl seen while walking the dog
  • hotspots with fewer than five checklists

How Night Mode overlaps with default Day Mode

The two modes share some checklists. Default Day Mode includes checklists starting from three hours before local sunrise up to, but not including, local sunset. Night Mode includes starts before sunrise or from one hour before sunset onwards. They therefore overlap for starts in the three hours before sunrise and the hour before sunset.

eBird records when a checklist started, not when each bird was seen, so Night Mode will pick up some species that were seen in daylight.

Nocturnal species are often recorded roosting during the day, and therefore will also appear in default Day Mode.

Group outings

When several people go birding together, one of them can share their eBird checklist with the others. Each member then has their own copy, which they can edit, for example by adding a bird only they saw or removing one they didn’t. So the copies of a shared checklist are mostly the same, but not always identical.

Both modes count each shared outing once. They differ in which species list stands for the outing:

  • Default Day Mode uses one member’s copy to represent the outing, including the species that member did not record.
  • Night Mode combines species across the copies that meet its checklist rules. A species counts once for the outing if any qualifying member’s copy reports it.
